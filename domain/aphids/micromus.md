# Domain — *Micromus variegatus* / *angulatus*

Brown lacewing. Cross-agent tables: `predators-comparison.md`.
Pest and program: `aphids.md`.


## Model


### What arrives

**Supplied as adults**, in a carton cup with buckwheat hulls and
honeycomb paper. The cup is **not a hatchery** — dividing it loses
nothing.

| | |
|---|---|
| Onset | **same day** — adults feed on arrival |
| Cohort life | adults live weeks and lay throughout |
| Feeding stages | **larva and adult** — the only agent on the list |
| Search | **flies**, finds colonies itself |
| Waves per 14-day cycle | **2** — adults, then their larvae |
| Temperature | active ~4–31 °C, no cold floor that matters here |
| Photoperiod | none — no diapause |
| Soil stage | none — lays on foliage |
| Shelf life | **48 h**, never refrigerated |
| Rate | **~1 /m² every 2 weeks** |

- North American product is *M. variegatus*; the European literature is *M. angulatus*. Same role

### Two waves inside one bed cycle

The mechanism that makes it attractive on a 14-day crop.

| Day | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0 | adults released, **feeding immediately** |
| 1–5 | adults lay in the crop |
| 5–12 | **larvae hatch and feed** |
| 14 | bed cut |

- **both stages are predatory** → the released adults are not egg-layers waiting to pay off, they eat from hour one

- the egg-to-feeding-larva step **fits inside 14 days**, so one release buys a second wave no single-stage agent provides

- no soil stage, no photoperiod requirement, no cold floor to respect


### Full life cycle (~20 °C)

| Day | Stage |
|---|---|
| 0 | adults released, feed immediately |
| 1–5 | pre-oviposition + laying |
| ~5–12 | eggs hatch → first-generation larvae (the second wave above) |
| — | larval stage ~10–14 d, pupa ~12–14 d |
| ~30–38 | new adults emerge |
| ~40–50 | their eggs hatch → **second-generation larvae** (~4–6 d pre-oviposition + ~6–8 d egg) |

- second generation ≈ **6–7 weeks after release at 20 °C**; nearer 25 °C the chain compresses to ~4–5 weeks


### What the research actually says

Greenhouse trial, aphid reduction against untreated control:

| Treatment | Reduction |
|---|---|
| *M. angulatus* **eggs** | 99.6 % |
| *C. carnea* **eggs** | 90.5 % |
| ***M. angulatus* adults** | **87.1 %** |

- the authors' own summary is **comparable to** *Chrysoperla*, not superior

- temperature reverses the ranking: at **20 °C *Chrysoperla* had the higher predation rate**; similar at 15 °C; *Micromus* pulls ahead nearer 25 °C. A lettuce house sits closer to 20

- third instars kill 30–52 aphids/day, so the appetite is real — it does not translate into better suppression at tested doses

- the literature-supported advantage is **sustained suppression over long periods**, because both stages feed


### Failure modes

- **the adults fly.** The delivered dose degrades by its own mobility — a share leaves for other bays or the glazing

- **48 h shelf life** makes every delivery a total-loss risk → the most fragile line per week of any option

- announced **stock-outs** at one supplier; a standing line cannot absorb a missing week


### Release quality

For a flier the levers are **when and how deep**, not how many
points.

- released on top of the canopy in daylight, adults fly off; released **into** the canopy at dusk, they settle

- open the cup at one bed end, walk it, sprinkle gently; the **honeycomb paper carries insects too** and is laid in the crop rather than discarded

- two or three points while walking is enough — 100 adults over 93 m² is a few metres of flight, and they redistribute within 24–72 h regardless

- consequence: spreading is about **even starting distribution**, not assigning insects to a bed. Do not build a procedure around it

- the one real gain from spreading is **lower escape pressure** — a heap in one spot pushes some to fly out of the zone


### Cadence: 100 fortnightly beats 50 weekly

| | 50 weekly | **100 fortnightly** |
|---|---|---|
| Insects per fortnight | 100 | 100 |
| Instantaneous density, 93 m² | 0.54 /m² | **1.08 /m²** |
| Against the label | half rate | **on rate** |
| Cost | $92.99/wk → 4 835/yr | $67.50/wk → **3 510/yr** |
| Reception events per year | 52 | **26** |

- **instantaneous density, not weekly throughput, is what the label sets.** Released adults must find each other to mate before they lay; halving density doubles the search distance and risks a low-density establishment failure

- **fortnightly halves exposure to the program's most fragile step** — 26 receptions carry half the accumulated risk of 52

- Conclusion: ***Weekly = Easier habit for the team***


### Verdict

The strongest mechanism on paper, unsupported by trial data at the
format sold, and 3.6× the cost of the lacewing cord. Its one real
edge — persistence — is the property a cut crop throws away.
