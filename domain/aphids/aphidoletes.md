# Domain — *Aphidoletes aphidimyza*

Predatory gall midge. Cross-agent tables: `predators-comparison.md`.
Pest and program: `aphids.md`.


## Model


### What arrives

**Supplied as pupae**, in moist vermiculite. The predator is the
**larva**, two steps away.

| | |
|---|---|
| Onset | **10–14 d** (Koppert quotes 14–21) |
| Cohort life | adult ~7–10 d; larva feeds 3–7 d |
| Feeding stages | larva only — adults take honeydew and nectar |
| Diet | obligate aphid specialist |
| Search | **best in class** — gravid females track honeydew volatiles |
| Reaches a lettuce heart | **yes**, larvae are small enough |
| Temperature floor | ~15 °C |
| Humidity | **> 70 % overnight**, when it emerges, mates and lays |
| Rate | preventive 0.25 /m²; light infestation 1–2 /m²; severe 5–10 /m² |
| Price | Koppert 1 000 pupae **$49.16**; NIC 1 000 **$78.99**; Anatis 1 000 **$99** |


### Why only pupae are sold

The pupa is the one stage that survives a box.

| Stage | Shippable? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Egg | no | microscopic, desiccates, laid singly inside a colony |
| Larva | no | 1–3 mm, soft, must feed continuously; pupates within days |
| **Pupa** | **yes** | quiescent in moist vermiculite, tolerates handling, emerges on arrival |
| Adult | no | fragile midge, ~1 week of life, does not feed on aphids |

- consequence: no supplier offers larvae or adults. The question is closed by the insect's biology, not by the catalogue

- **pupae are also the cheap format**, ~$0.05 each — the cheapest live agent after lacewing eggs. Rearing cost accumulates with each stage carried, so the later the stage the dearer

- consequence: the program's cost is not the format. It is that each pupa buys one adult, and that adult may or may not find a colony


### The shipped stage is not the predatory stage

- predation is **two steps removed** from delivery, and the middle step is a **search**

- consequence: that is why Koppert labels it a hotspot tool and quotes 14–21 d

- consequence: there is no way to deploy it that skips the find-a-colony step. Any program using it bets that colonies exist

- **the bet is safe at Décembre** — the house is not clean. Koppert's warning describes releasing into a sterile crop

- consequence: it is a **responsive amplifier, not a preventive floor**. It works in proportion to what there is to find, and the right time to revisit it is if colonies ever become genuinely rare


### Diapause

A **larval** state, entered at cocoon-spinning **after feeding is
finished**.

```
pupa (bought) → adult → egg → LARVA FEEDS → larva drops to soil
   → spins cocoon → [diapause decision] → pupa → adult
```

- consequence: **diapause does not stop a larva from eating aphids.** It stops that individual from becoming an adult

- consequence: a purchased cohort delivers, under any daylength, adults that lay and **one full larval generation that feeds**. Short days remove the *second* generation of adults

- consequence: light is a **multiplier on generations**, never a precondition for the predation already paid for

- diapause-destined larvae feed a little longer to build reserves → the short-day cohort is not a weaker feeder

- breaking diapause needs weeks of chilling → a diapaused cohort is lost for **months**, not delayed by days. The failure is not gradual


### Reproduction, if you want an F1

**Photoperiod ≥ 16 h, every day.**

- the cue is **day length, not brightness** — a bright 9 h winter day induces diapause exactly as a dull one does

- the decision is made by the **larva** over its ~4–7 d feeding period → a single long day does not rescue a short-day week

- the signal is a dark-period length and insect receptors saturate at very low intensity → **a few µmol/m²/s extends the photoperiod as well as full crop lighting**

- consequence: day-extension is decoupled from crop lighting — a low-wattage circuit, negligible energy, negligible added DLI

- warm temperature (> 20 °C) suppresses diapause incidence even under marginal daylength

- **non-diapausing strains** are sold by some suppliers → sourcing one removes the constraint

**Moist, untilled soil** for pupation, top 1–2 cm, 10–14 d.

- surface disturbance from pulling and transplanting is survivable; **tilling, inversion, or letting the surface dry is not**

- consequence: on a fast bed-turnover cycle pupation cannot rely on bed soil. It needs a **permanent undisturbed moist refuge** — pathway soil, bed edges, or a dedicated strip

- long-season tomato beds supply this for nothing: the 10 in exposed strip is bare, drip-irrigated and untilled all season

**Free summer window.** Quebec City exceeds 16 h natural daylight
~mid-May to early-August → in that window an F1 costs nothing and
needs no installation.

- it will not reproduce in the lettuce beds even then — 2-week flips destroy the pupal window


### Lighting a reservoir zone rather than the whole house

A lit sub-zone can breed for the rest of the house, but only if it
satisfies **all three** at once.

| Requirement | Why |
|---|---|
| ≥ 16 h light, every day | otherwise the F1 diapauses |
| Moist untilled soil | larvae drop and pupate in the top 1–2 cm |
| **Standing aphid colony** | females only lay into colonies; a clean zone produces nothing |

- **the prey requirement is the binding one** — a reservoir zone must carry aphids deliberately and continuously

- consequence: a fast-turnover crop bed is the wrong host — it fails the soil requirement and carrying aphids on purpose in a saleable crop is unacceptable

- consequence: a long-season crop that already hosts the pest and already has moist untilled soil is the right host. The reservoir costs only the light

- a cereal + *Rhopalosiphum padi* unit is the safe alternative host, but reintroduces a live colony to maintain

- adults are **weak, night-flying dispersers** → one reservoir does not seed a large multi-bay house evenly. Coverage argues for one zone per bay


### Release conditions — the mechanism, not hygiene

**The container is a hatchery**, not a packet. Adults emerge over
several days, at dusk each day.

- consequence: the conditions that matter are the ones the container sits in for the next 3–5 days, not the instant it was carried out

- consequence: "release at dawn or dusk" is borrowed from agents you *scatter*. For a hatchery it is much weaker advice

| Failure | Fixed by real canopy shade? |
|---|---|
| Radiant heating of the container | **yes** — sits near air temperature, not 40 °C |
| Emergence into bright light, dispersal to glazing | **largely** |
| Desiccation of the carrier | **no** — vapour pressure deficit peaks at midday regardless |

- consequence: **under a dense tomato canopy, midday placement is fine.** The residual risk is the carrier drying, which misting answers

- consequence: over open lettuce beds there is no canopy shade → timing or a physical cover is the only protection

- place **open and upright**; never tip or scatter the carrier

- mist if dry, and **check again mid-week** — one misting at release does not carry an emitter working for five days

- **never on a bed cut within a week** — the cohort needs 10–14 days and the container leaves with the harvest


### Failure modes

- **released into a clean crop it is a total loss.** Females need a colony; larvae eat aphids only

- the search is **density-dependent** — a five-aphid colony emits almost no honeydew, so the colonies easiest to kill early are the hardest to find

- heat, desiccation and bright light at release destroy an emergence that placement was supposed to deliver

- **dose is unverifiable at delivery** without an emergence test —
  pupae in vermiculite cannot be inspected; only adults flying
  days later prove the shipment was alive

- sulphur, sticky cards, and spray residues each remove a share of every cohort
  → `aphids.md § Silent killers of a release`


### Verdict

Cheapest per insect, best searcher, slowest onset. Right for a
long-season crop where a 10–14 d lag costs nothing. Wrong as the
sole agent on a bed cut every 14 days.
